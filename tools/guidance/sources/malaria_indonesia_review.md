# What to Do in a High-Risk Malaria Mosquito Zone: An Evidence Review for Students (Indonesia Focus)

The best-proven action in a high-risk malaria zone is to sleep every night under an insecticide-treated net (ITN), and to get a malaria blood test the same day anyone in the house gets a fever. Other measures add to these two but do not replace them. Next in line are house screening, a WHO-recommended spatial emanator indoors, and community measures timed to the rainy season.\[7\]\[40\]

*This report is for information and education only. It is not medical advice. Anyone with a fever in or after visiting a malaria area should see a health worker (puskesmas/clinic) promptly.*

## TL;DR

- **Nets plus fast testing come first.** Cochrane reviews (high-certainty evidence) find ITNs cut child deaths by 17%, severe malaria by 44%, and uncomplicated *P. falciparum* episodes by almost half (rate ratio 0.55) compared with no nets.\[7\] A same-day blood test and full artemisinin-based treatment (ACT) for any fever prevents deaths and stops further spread.\[40\]
- **Add household and community layers, matched to how the local mosquito behaves.** Screening and other house modifications probably reduce malaria infection (Cochrane, moderate certainty). Since August 2025, WHO conditionally recommends indoor spatial emanators on top of nets. Larviciding and draining habitats help mainly where breeding sites are "few, fixed and findable". Skin repellents give only a small extra benefit, but are useful in the evening and outdoors.\[3\]\[6\]
- **In Indonesia the risk is concentrated in Tanah Papua, and the mosquitoes differ from dengue mosquitoes.** Kemenkes recorded 543,965 malaria cases in 2024 (up from 418,546 in 2023). A 2025 Nature Communications commentary counts 527,381 confirmed cases, "93 percent of which originated from Papua Island." *Anopheles* bite mainly from dusk to dawn and breed in sunlit pools, swamps, rice fields and brackish coastal lagoons. Dengue's *Aedes* bite in the daytime and breed in containers. For an acoustic app, peer-reviewed work (Stanford Abuzz, Oxford HumBug) shows phone microphones can pick up mosquito wingbeats, though identifying *Anopheles* is harder than identifying other species.\[57\]\[58\]\[56\]

## Details

### 1. Personal protection

**Insecticide-treated nets (strongest evidence).** The Cochrane review by Pryce, Richardson and Lengeler (2018) found that ITNs cut uncomplicated *P. falciparum* episodes by almost half compared with no nets (rate ratio 0.55, 95% CI 0.48–0.64; 5 trials, 35,551 participants, high certainty).\[7\]\[8\] They also cut child mortality by 17% and severe malaria by 44%.\[1\] ITNs probably reduce *P. vivax* episodes too (RR 0.61). Treated nets still beat untreated nets (rate ratio 0.58),\[7\] which suggests they have stayed effective despite growing insecticide resistance.\[9\] Kemenkes advises that nets work best when community coverage is above 80% and they are used correctly: tuck the bottom edges under the mattress, check regularly for holes and sew them up.\[10\] Because nets also kill mosquitoes, high community use protects neighbours as well.\[11\]

**Timing of exposure.** *Anopheles* bite mainly between dusk and dawn, but local vectors vary. In Jayapura, Papua, researchers found *An. farauti* 4 biting early in the evening and indoors.\[12\] In the Solomon Islands, *An. farauti* shifted to biting early in the evening outdoors after insecticide campaigns.\[13\] In practical terms, the riskiest hours are the evening hours before going to bed (sitting outside, fishing, night work). This is when repellents, long clothing and spatial emanators fill the gap that nets leave.

**Topical repellents.** The 2023 Cochrane review (Gabaldón Figueira et al.) found that repellents "may slightly reduce" *P. falciparum* infection and clinical cases (pooled IRR 0.74; low certainty). They "may make little or no difference" where ITNs and other measures are already available.\[6\] The benefit was clearest among refugees and others without access to nets. Side effects were rare (about 0.6% of users).\[14\]\[15\] DEET, picaridin (icaridin), IR3535 and PMD (oil of lemon eucalyptus) all repel *Anopheles* well, but none has strong evidence of preventing malaria on its own. Treat them as an evening and outdoor supplement, not a substitute for nets.

**Treated clothing.** The 2018 Cochrane review (Maia et al.) found that where nets were not used, insecticide-treated clothing may reduce clinical *P. vivax* (RR 0.64, low certainty).\[16\] The evidence comes mostly from soldiers and refugees.

**Spatial repellents/emanators (newest WHO tool).** On 13 August 2025, WHO issued a conditional recommendation for indoor spatial emanators in addition to ITNs. It rests on moderate-certainty evidence from 5 studies and is described as the first new vector control product class in more than 40 years.\[17\]\[18\] WHO advises using prequalified products (the first two prequalified were SC Johnson's transfluthrin emanators, Mosquito Shield and Guardian).\[19\]\[20\]\[21\] A review of 61 studies (Chen, Moore et al., eBioMedicine 2025) found that spatial repellents gave an average 56% protection against mosquito bites, and coils alone gave 46% (95% CI 36–56%).

Indonesia hosted key trials. In Sumba, NTT, a 2014 study of coils found 52% protection.\[22\]\[23\] The larger 2020 cluster-randomized trial of transfluthrin emanators (Syafruddin et al.) found a 27.7–31.3% protective effect across all 24 clusters, which was not statistically significant.\[22\] Among 12 moderate- to high-risk clusters, protection rose to a significant 60%.\[24\] In plain terms, emanators help most where transmission is intense. Ordinary coils and plug-in devices are not the same as the prequalified products that were tested.

### 2. Household measures

**House screening and modifications.** The 2022 Cochrane review (Fox et al.) covered 7 trials, all in sub-Saharan Africa. Screening windows, doors and eaves (sometimes with metal roofs or eave tubes) probably reduced malaria parasite prevalence (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.57–0.82; moderate certainty). It also reduced moderate-to-severe anaemia (RR 0.70; high certainty). Effects on clinical malaria were inconsistent: individual trials ranged from 62% fewer cases to more cases, and certainty was very low. Eave tubes are a "lure and kill" device that lets air through while killing mosquitoes.\[3\] WHO (2021) conditionally recommends house screening.\[25\] For a typical Indonesian house, fitting kawat kasa (wire mesh) to vents and windows and closing gaps under the roof are cheap, practical steps.

**Indoor residual spraying (IRS).** IRS means the health programme sprays long-lasting insecticide on inside walls.\[4\]\[26\] The Cochrane review (Choi, Pryce & Garner 2019; updated by Pryce et al. 2022) found that adding IRS with non-pyrethroid insecticides to ITNs was associated with lower malaria prevalence, although results varied between trials. Adding pyrethroid IRS showed no extra benefit.\[27\]\[28\]\[29\] IRS is a programme decision, not a household DIY task. Kemenkes technical guidance notes that villages receiving IRS generally should not also receive nets, because both rely on insecticides.\[30\] IRS works best against mosquitoes that rest indoors. Papua's vectors often bite and rest outdoors, which reduces IRS impact there.

### 3. Community and environmental measures (larval source management)

**Where *Anopheles* breed.** *Anopheles* larvae prefer clean, often sunlit, ground water: swamps, rice fields, ponds, stream edges, hoof prints, and, along Indonesia's coasts, brackish lagoons. *An. sundaicus*, a major vector in western Indonesia and in coastal NTT/Sumba, is "typically associated with coastal, sunlit, brackish water habitats, and often with floating algal mats".\[31\]\[32\] This is very different from dengue's *Aedes aegypti*, which breeds in clean water inside containers (bak mandi, buckets, tyres).\[33\] Kemenkes' 3M Plus campaign (Menguras, Menutup, Mendaur ulang/Mengubur, plus nets and repellents) was designed for *Aedes*.\[34\]\[35\] Container clean-ups alone will not touch most *Anopheles* habitats.

**Does it work?** The Cochrane larviciding review (Choi, Majambere & Wilson 2019) concluded: "Ground larviciding for non‑extensive larval habitats may have an effect on malaria transmission, and we do not know if there is an effect in large‑scale aquatic habitats."\[5\] In one cluster trial, larviciding was associated with 76% lower incidence (rate ratio 0.24; low certainty).\[36\] A trial in Gambia, where marshes stretched for kilometres, found no effect.\[5\] WHO's current guideline makes larviciding a conditional recommendation (low certainty). It is a supplement to ITNs or IRS "where aquatic habitats are few, fixed and findable", and WHO warns it "should not be seen as a substitute for ITNs or IRS."\[37\]\[38\]

**When it works best.** Community action makes most sense in these situations:
- Habitats are limited and mappable: an urban edge, a village with a few lagoons or fishponds, or the dry season when water shrinks to a few pools.
- After floods, when new pools appear near houses. Fill or drain them early, since larvae develop in about 1–2 weeks.
- Abandoned fishponds or lagoons are being re-opened to the sea, flushed, or cleared of algae mats (for *An. sundaicus*).
- Bti (a biological larvicide) is used under health-office guidance.

Kemenkes guidance calls for sentinel larval surveys in coastal, rice-field, hill and forest settings, and for updating receptive-area data twice a year according to season.\[30\]

### 4. Medical measures

**Recognize symptoms and test early.** Malaria typically starts with fever, chills, headache and body aches. It can resemble flu or dengue, so it cannot be diagnosed by symptoms alone. Incubation is usually 9–14 days for *P. falciparum* and 12–17 days for *P. vivax* (Kemenkes 2013 guideline).\[39\] A fever up to weeks or months after a visit to a malaria area should therefore prompt a test. Kemenkes and WHO recommend ACT (e.g. artemether-lumefantrine) for confirmed malaria; severe cases need hospital injection treatment.\[40\] CDC stresses that all febrile people returning from malaria areas "should be tested for malaria even if they took chemoprophylaxis."\[41\] *P. vivax* can hide in the liver and relapse, so finishing primaquine/radical cure as prescribed matters.

**Chemoprophylaxis for travellers.** CDC's Yellow Book 2026 lists atovaquone-proguanil, doxycycline, mefloquine or tafenoquine for chloroquine-resistant areas.\[42\] Atovaquone-proguanil and doxycycline start 1–2 days before travel, which suits last-minute trips. Atovaquone-proguanil continues 7 days after leaving and doxycycline 4 weeks after.\[41\]\[43\] Doxycycline is not for pregnant women or children under 8, and tafenoquine requires a G6PD test.\[41\]\[44\] A doctor must choose the right drug.

**Seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC).** SMC means monthly antimalarial courses for young children during the rainy season. In a 2025 review of Sahel programmes, Djedanem et al. (PLOS Global Public Health) report reductions in malaria incidence reaching 87.4%. A separate meta-analysis found SMC was 61% effective against clinical malaria 29–42 days after a dose. It is recommended for areas of highly seasonal transmission, mainly Africa's Sahel, not Indonesia.

**Vaccines.** WHO recommended RTS,S/AS01 in 2021 and R21/Matrix-M in October 2023 for children in endemic areas.\[45\] In the R21 phase 3 trial in 4,644 African children aged 5–36 months, efficacy was 75% at seasonal sites and 68% at perennial sites over 12 months.\[46\] WHO states that both vaccines "prevent around 75% of malaria episodes when given seasonally."\[2\] Both target *P. falciparum* only and are deployed in African programmes. They are not currently part of Indonesia's programme, where *P. vivax* is also common.

### 5. Early warning, surveillance and AI detection

**Climate-based prediction.** A 2025 scoping review in PLOS Global Public Health found 30 malaria early warning system studies from 16 countries. Most used rainfall and temperature, usually with time-series models. Translating forecasts into action remains the weak point.\[47\]\[48\] Lags matter: in Senegal, malaria cases peak about two months after the August rainfall peak, reflecting mosquito breeding time plus human incubation.\[49\] Machine-learning studies commonly use lagged rainfall, lagged minimum temperature and vegetation index (NDVI) as top predictors.\[50\] Kemenkes notes that high transmission usually occurs in the rainy season.\[30\] The practical rule is to step up nets, screening and testing at the start of the rains and in the 1–2 months after heavy rain or floods.

**Acoustic detection.** Female mosquitoes' wingbeat "whine" differs by species.\[51\]
- **Stanford Abuzz** (Mukundarajan, Hol, Castillo, Newby & Prakash, *eLife* 2017) showed that even cheap flip-phones can record species-specific wingbeat frequencies. The team built a reference set of 20 medically important species and mapped mosquitoes in California and a Madagascar village using volunteers trained for about 15 minutes.\[52\]\[53\]
- **Oxford HumBug** (Sinka et al., *Methods in Ecology and Evolution* 2021; Kiskin et al., HumBugDB, NeurIPS 2021) uses budget Android phones running the MozzWear app inside adapted bednets (HumBug Nets). Audio is recorded at 8 kHz. A pipeline removes human speech, detects mosquito events and then classifies species.\[54\]\[55\] HumBug's earlier paper noted that detection accuracy for *Anopheles* was "impressive albeit worse" than for other species.\[56\]

For a hackathon app, the open HumBugDB dataset and the two-step design (detection first, then species classification) are a good starting point. Be honest that a buzz detector measures mosquito presence, not malaria risk, and should point users to nets and testing.

### 6. Indonesia and Southeast Asia context

**Where malaria remains.**
- Indonesia recorded about 543,965 cases in 2024 (Kemenkes figure reported by detik and a 2025 PMC study), up about 30% from 418,546 in 2023.\[57\] A Nature Communications 2025 commentary cites 527,381 confirmed cases; the counts differ slightly by source and date.\[58\]
- More than 90% of cases (about 93%) come from Tanah Papua, which has only about 2% of the population. By Kemenkes figures reported by Kompas, Papua Province had about 229,000 cases, Central Papua 168,000 and South Papua 57,000.\[59\]\[60\]
- Keerom, Jayapura, Mimika, Sarmi and other lowland districts carry the heaviest burden. In some villages, malaria incidence can exceed 500 per 1,000 people each year (Nature Communications 2025).
- NTT (including Sumba) and parts of Maluku and Kalimantan still have transmission.
- 407 of 514 districts were malaria-free in 2024 (Nature Communications 2025). Kemenkes's latest update puts the figure at 412 of 514 districts/cities (about 80%), plus seven provinces. The national target is elimination by 2030.
- Kemenkes's Director-General of Disease Control, Murti Utami, said WHO estimates about 1.1 million cases in Indonesia and "we have only found 54 percent" ("kita baru menemukan 54 persen"), so active testing matters.

**Main vectors.** The number of confirmed vector species depends on the source: 20 (Elyazar et al. 2013), 26 (the Kemenkes National Action Plan 2020–2026), or at least 31 (a 2024 biodiversity review).
- In Papua, the *An. punctulatus* group (*An. farauti*, *An. koliensis*, *An. punctulatus*) dominates. Some members bite early and outdoors, and pyrethroid resistance is emerging.\[61\]
- In coastal western and central Indonesia and NTT, *An. sundaicus* breeds in brackish lagoons.\[32\]\[61\]
- Others include *An. balabacensis* in forests (also linked to zoonotic *P. knowlesi*), *An. aconitus* in rice fields, and *An. barbirostris*/*An. subpictus*.

**Local guidance.** Kemenkes promotes free insecticide-treated nets (mass campaigns in high-endemic districts), IRS in selected villages, repellents, mass blood surveys, prompt ACT treatment, and 3M Plus.\[11\]\[35\]\[59\]

**How this differs from dengue.**

| | Malaria (*Anopheles*) | Dengue (*Aedes aegypti/albopictus*) |
|---|---|---|
| Biting time | Dusk to dawn (some early evening, outdoors) | Daytime (morning and late afternoon) |
| Breeding | Ground water: sunlit pools, swamps, rice fields, brackish lagoons | Clean water in containers around homes |
| Key tool | Bed nets at night + testing/ACT | Removing containers (3M), daytime repellent |
| Pathogen | *Plasmodium* parasite (treatable with drugs) | Virus (no specific antiviral) |
| Where in Indonesia | Mostly Papua, NTT, eastern islands | Nationwide, especially cities |

Bed nets matter less for dengue because *Aedes* bite during the day. Container clean-ups matter less for malaria because *Anopheles* rarely breed in containers.

## Recommendations: What to Do When You Are in a High-Danger Zone

**Individuals (every day, all year, more strictly in the rainy season):**
1. Sleep under an intact, tucked-in insecticide-treated net every night, including children and pregnant women.\[7\]
2. From dusk onward, wear long sleeves and trousers and use a registered repellent (DEET, picaridin, IR3535 or PMD) on exposed skin, especially when outdoors in the evening.\[6\]
3. Get a malaria test the same day for any fever, and finish the full treatment course.\[40\]
4. If you are visiting Papua or another high-risk area, see a doctor 1–2 weeks before the trip about chemoprophylaxis.

**Households:**
1. Fit wire mesh (kawat kasa) to windows and vents, close eave gaps and repair holes.\[3\]
2. Use a WHO-prequalified spatial emanator indoors where available, in addition to (not instead of) nets.\[37\]
3. Accept IRS when the health programme offers it.\[27\]

**Communities and schools:**
1. Map and remove standing ground water near homes: puddles, abandoned ponds, blocked drains, hoof prints, and pools left after floods.\[5\]
2. Ask the puskesmas about larviciding (e.g. Bti) for permanent lagoons and fishponds.\[37\]
3. Time campaigns to the start of the rainy season and the 1–2 months after heavy rains.\[30\]

**For the hackathon project:**
1. Train on HumBugDB and Abuzz-style wingbeat features.
2. Add a "dusk-to-dawn risk mode" for *Anopheles*.
3. Combine detection with local rainfall data.
4. Make the app's output an action (use your net tonight, get tested if you have a fever), not just a mosquito count.

## Caveats

- Most trial evidence for house screening, IRS, SMC and vaccines comes from sub-Saharan Africa, where vectors bite mainly indoors late at night. Papua's vectors often bite early and outdoors, so indoor tools may protect less there, and personal outdoor protection matters more.\[3\]
- Evidence for repellents, treated clothing and larviciding is low certainty. These measures can reduce bites without clearly showing fewer malaria cases.\[6\]
- The Sumba spatial repellent trial was not statistically significant overall. Its 60% figure comes from a subgroup of higher-risk clusters.\[24\]\[62\]
- Indonesian case counts differ slightly by source (527,381 vs 543,965 for 2024). True numbers are higher: according to Kemenkes, only 54% of WHO's estimated 1.1 million cases nationally have been found.\[57\]\[58\]
- Insecticide resistance (pyrethroids) and partial artemisinin resistance (Greater Mekong) are growing threats, so local programme advice can change.\[59\]
- Acoustic AI detection is a research tool. It has not yet been shown to reduce malaria cases, and identifying *Anopheles* by sound is less accurate than identifying other species.\[56\]
- This report is informational, not medical advice. Drug choices and doses must come from a qualified health professional.
